Stars

Stars form when gravity pulls a cloud of gas together. In a main sequence star, nuclear fusion in the core joins hydrogen nuclei to make helium and releases energy. The hot gas produces outward pressure that balances gravity.

A star's color tells us about its surface temperature. Blue stars have hotter surfaces than red stars. How bright a star looks from Earth also depends on its distance, so apparent brightness alone does not tell us its temperature or total light output.

Stellar Life Cycles

A star's mass strongly affects its lifetime. Massive main sequence stars use their fuel much faster than smaller stars, so they have shorter lives. When the Sun runs out of hydrogen in its core, it will eventually become a red giant and leave a white dwarf. Much more massive stars can end in supernovae and leave neutron stars or black holes.

More Practice

Question: A red star looks brighter than a blue star from Earth. Does that mean the red star has a hotter surface?
answer

No. Blue stars have hotter surfaces than red stars. Apparent brightness also depends on distance and total light output.

Example: Sirius is about 8.6 light-years from Earth and has a surface temperature of about 10 000 K. How much energy does Sirius emit in one second?
solution

The distance and temperature are not enough. We need Sirius's total power output, or enough additional information and an equation to calculate it. A star's size affects its total light output too.

Question: Why can a massive main sequence star have a shorter life even though it starts with more fuel?
answer

It uses its fuel much faster. Having more fuel does not guarantee a longer lifetime if the rate of fuel use is much larger.
