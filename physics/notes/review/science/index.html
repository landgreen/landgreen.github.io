Hypothesis, Theory, and Law

In science the words hypothesis, theory, and law have special meanings that don't exactly agree with how they are used outside of science.

Hypotheses, theories and laws aren't facts. Science doesn't produce unchanging truths. Science is an ongoing process of discovery, and each new explanation has the potential to improve on the previous.


A phenomenon is an observable event, like the orbits of planets, static electricity, and lactose intolerance.

A hypothesis is a possible explanation for a phenomenon.

A scientific theory is a hypothesis that has been tested many times and never been proven false. Theories are explanations. They answer the question: why does this phenomenon occur? Examples include: quantum field theory, big bang theory, evolution, and germ theory.


A scientific law is also based on the results of many tests, but a law doesn't explain why a phenomenon occurs. Instead, laws are a general description of what has happened and what will happen for a narrow range of conditions.
  • Laws predict the behavior of a natural phenomenon.
  • Laws are only accurate for a limited range of conditions.
  • Laws are typically a mathematical equation, or a rule.

    • Almost all laws are found in the physical sciences. Some examples are: Ohm's Law, Universal Gravitation, Coulomb's law, and Kirchhoff's laws.

    After observing the motion of the planets, Nicolaus Copernicus published the idea that the planets revolved around the sun. This idea was tested many times and never disproven. Sixty years later, Johannes Kepler discovered an equation that predicted how the planets moved around the sun.

    Probability and Truth

    A probability expresses how strongly the evidence supports a claim. New evidence can change that probability; a high probability is not the same as certainty.

    Scientists report uncertainty rather than claiming that every conclusion is certain. For a testable claim, measurements and repeated experiments can strengthen or weaken the evidence.

    Human Biases

    Science uses measurements, repeatable methods, and independent checks to reduce the influence of human biases.

    We tend to interpret the world from our own point of view. Measurements can show when that viewpoint is misleading. For example, velocity must be measured relative to a stated reference frame.

    Common cognitive biases include confirmation bias: paying more attention to evidence that agrees with what we already think.

    Perception and Reality

    Many things we study, such as electric fields and subatomic particles, cannot be sensed directly. We infer their properties from measurable effects.

    Our senses can be misleading. Metal and wood at the same temperature can feel different because they transfer heat at different rates.

    A strong impression or personal belief is not enough to establish that something exists. A scientific explanation needs evidence that others can check.

    "Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away."
    - Philip K. Dick

    Modeling of Reality

    Science builds models to explain an aspect of reality. Most models are only accurate within a range of conditions.

    Newtonian physics is a model that explains how things move, but it isn't a perfect description of reality. Relativistic physics is a more accurate model of reality near the speed of light, and quantum mechanics is more accurate for very short distances. No current scientific model can describe our universe perfectly.

    Pseudoscience

    A claim is not scientific merely because it uses scientific words. Ask whether it makes testable predictions, accounts for conflicting evidence, and can be checked independently.

    Quantification

    A quantitative description gives a number and usually a unit, such as a temperature of 20°C. A qualitative description uses words, such as “warm.” Numbers make comparisons and predictions easier to check.

    Shared units and clear definitions let different people compare measurements. Report the precision of a measurement as well as its value.
