Motion

Physics makes models that approximate reality with math. Physics models have made amazing progress, but so far no model perfectly matches reality.

This model approximates particle motion and collisions. It gives each particle a position and velocity. Every second, a particle's velocity is added to its position. Also, since this is a 2-D model, the calculations are repeated in the horizontal and vertical.

Question: Imagine this is a simulation of billiards or pool. What does this simulation need to be more realistic?
answer

  • there could be pockets
  • balls that are the same size
  • balls that can roll
  • air friction
  • rolling friction
  • when the balls collide they make a sound
  • collisions that lose energy
  • a 3rd dimension, so the balls can bounce
  • gravity
  • walls that aren't perfectly rigid rectangles

    At the extremes we'd also need things like:
  • slightly bumpy shaped balls, not perfect spheres
  • quantum effects, like tunneling
  • effects of relativity, like time dilation

    • Position

    We use the symbol x for position with meters for units. You might be used to using x in other ways, but in physics it is reserved for position.

    To represent a displacement we subtract the initial position from the final position. We use the Δ symbol to show change.

    $$ \Delta x = x_f-x_i$$

    \(\Delta x\) = change in position, displacement [m, meters] vector

    \(x_i\) = initial position [m, meters] vector

    \(x_f\) = final position [m, meters] vector

    Example: Mount Everest is 8848 m above sea level. The base camp, where most mountain climbers begin their trek, is 5150 m above sea level. What is the vertical distance between the base camp and the peak?
    solution $$ \text{final position} = 8848\,\mathrm{m} $$ $$\text{initial position} = 5150\,\mathrm{m}$$
    $$ \Delta x = x_{f}-x_{i}$$ $$ \Delta x = 8848\,\mathrm{m}-5150\,\mathrm{m}$$ $$ \Delta x = 3698\,\mathrm{m}$$
    Example: Convert your answer from the previous problem into miles and kilometers. Which is easier to convert?
    1 mile = 1609 meters
    1 kilometer = 1000 meters
    solution $$ \Delta x = 3698\,\mathrm{m}$$
    $$ 3698\,\mathrm{m} \left( \mathrm{ \frac{1 \, mile}{1609.34 \, m}} \right) = 2.297 \, \mathrm{mile} $$
    $$3698\,\mathrm{m} = 3\overleftarrow{\undergroup{6}\undergroup{9}\undergroup{8}}. \, \mathrm{m}= 3.698 \,\mathrm{km}$$
    Distance traveled refers to the length of a path.


    Displacement is a change in position. The path taken is irrelevant.

    My school is only 1.5 km from where I live. I'm able to ride my bike most days. One morning I had biked half a kilometer before I realized I forgot my lunch. So, I rode home, grabbed my lunch, and rushed to school.

    Example: What's my displacement?
    solution

    Displacement is final position minus initial position. Heading backwards, going slower, or taking a different path doesn't change its value.

    $$ \Delta x = x_f-x_i$$ $$ \Delta x = 1.5 \, \mathrm{km}-0$$ $$ \Delta x = 1.5 \, \mathrm{km}$$
    Example: What's my distance traveled?
    solution

    Distance traveled measures the length of the path. It will include heading back home to get my lunch.

    $$d = 0.5 \, \mathrm{km} + 0.5 \, \mathrm{km} + 1.5 \, \mathrm{km}$$ $$d = 2.5 \, \mathrm{km}$$

    Time

    We keep track of time in the same way as position. We use t as the symbol with seconds as our main unit.

    $$\Delta t = t_{f}-t_{i}$$

    \(\Delta t\) = change in time, time period [s, seconds]

    \(t_i\) = initial time [s, seconds]

    \(t_f\) = final time [s, seconds]

    Example: School ends at 2:30 PM. I got home today at 2:49 PM. How long did it take me to get home in minutes?
    solution

    We can just focus on the minutes, since the hours didn't change.

    $$\Delta t = t_{f}-t_{i}$$ $$\Delta t = 49\,\mathrm{min}-30\, \mathrm{min}$$ $$\Delta t = 19\, \mathrm{min}$$

    Example: My school starts early at 7:30 am. Each class is 1 hour long with 3 minutes between classes. What time is it at the end of the second period?

    You can probably solve this in your head, but try using Δt = tf - ti for practice.
    solution $$\Delta t = 1\, \mathrm{hr} + 1 \, \mathrm{hr} + 3\, \mathrm{min}$$ $$\Delta t = 2\, \mathrm{hr} + 3\, \mathrm{min}$$
    $$\Delta t = t_{f}-t_{i}$$ $$t_{f} = \Delta t + t_{i}$$ $$t_{f} = (2\, \mathrm{hr} + 3\, \mathrm{min}) + (7\, \mathrm{hr} + 30\, \mathrm{min})$$ $$t_{f} = 9\, \mathrm{hr} + 33\, \mathrm{min}$$

    Velocity

    Velocity measures how much position changes, Δx, over a period of time, Δt.

    $$v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$

    \(\Delta x\) = change in position, displacement [m, meters] vector

    \(\Delta t\) = time period [s, seconds]

    \(v\) = average velocity [m/s, meters per second] vector

    Example: A car is traveling at an average of 2 m/s for 80 seconds. How far does the car travel?
    solution $$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$2 = \frac{\Delta x}{80}$$ $$(2)(80) = \Delta x$$ $$160\,\mathrm{m} = \Delta x$$
    Question: Why are we solving for average velocity? Why not just velocity?
    answer

    The velocity might change over the journey. What if someone speeds up or slows down? There are many possible velocities that fit a given distance and time, but only one average velocity.

    In the next unit we will learn how to find the instantaneous velocity.

    Example: Someone tells you they can run 10 km in about an hour. What velocity is that in m/s? Is that fast? (1 m/s is walking speed)
    solution $$10 \, \mathrm{km} = 10(1000) \mathrm{m} = \color{#f07} 10\,000 \, \mathrm{m}$$ $$1\, \mathrm{h} \left(\frac{60\, \mathrm{min}}{1\, \mathrm{h}}\right)\left(\frac{60\, \mathrm{s}}{1\, \mathrm{min}}\right) = \color{#09d} 3600 \,\mathrm{s}$$
    $$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v = \frac{\color{#f07}10\,000\, \mathrm{m}}{\color{#09d} 3600\, \mathrm{s}}$$ $$v = 2.7\, \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$
    Example: Google maps says Las Vegas is 4 hours away from Los Angeles. Google says it is 270 miles away. How fast does google think I will drive? Answer this one in miles/hour.
    solution $$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v = \frac{270\, \mathrm{miles} }{4\, \mathrm{hour}}$$ $$v = 67.5\, \mathrm{\tfrac{miles}{hour} }$$

    The posted speed limit is 70 mph.

    Question: Imagine you are in a plane with your eyes closed. Can you feel when the plane is increasing or decreasing altitude? What does it feel like?
    answer

    A plane dropping at a constant 100 m/s would feel the same as a plane staying at the same altitude. There is no way to directly measure your velocity relative to the Earth, but you can indirectly guess your velocity by looking outside, or measuring air pressure.

    You can feel acceleration, the change in velocity. For me, accelerating down feels like butterflies, and accelerating up feels like pressure.

    Example: If I walk at a speed of 1.2 m/s how long will it take for me to walk 2 km?
    solution $$2 \, \mathrm{km} = 2(1000) \mathrm{m} = 2000 \, \mathrm{m}$$
    $$v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$1.2 = \frac{2000}{\Delta t}$$ $$1.2\Delta t = 2000$$ $$\Delta t = \frac{2000}{1.2}$$ $$\Delta t = 1666.\overline{6} \, \mathrm{s}$$

    velocity

    Acceleration

    Acceleration is a measure of how much velocity changes (Δv) over a period of time (Δt).

    $$a_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

    \(\Delta v\) = change in velocity [m/s] = \(v_f-v_i\) vector

    \(\Delta t\) = time period, change in time [s, seconds]

    \(a\) = acceleration [m/s²] vector

    Example: A basketball falls off a table and hits the floor in 0.45 s. The ball has a velocity of 4.43 m/s right before it hits the ground. What is the acceleration of the basketball as it falls?
    solution $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a = \frac{4.43\, \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}} {0.45\, \mathrm{s}}$$ $$a = 9.84\, \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}} }$$
    Example: A Porsche Taycan Turbo GT (2025) can go from 0 to 60 miles per hour in 1.898 s. What is the acceleration in m/s²? (1 mile = 1609 meters)
    strategy Convert 60 miles into meters and hours into seconds, then use the acceleration equation. Since initial velocity is zero the Δv becomes just v. $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a = \frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$a = \frac{v_f}{\Delta t}$$
    solution $$ 60\left( \mathrm{ \frac{\color{#f05}{mile}}{\color{#07b}{hour}}} \right)\left(\frac{1609\,\mathrm{m}}{1 \,\color{#f05}{\mathrm{mile} }}\right)\left(\frac{1\, \color{#07b}{ \mathrm{hour} }}{3600\,\mathrm{s} }\right) = 26.8 \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}} $$ $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a = \frac{26.8\,\mathrm{ \tfrac{m}{s}}} {1.898\, \mathrm{s}}$$ $$a = 14.1\, \mathrm{ \tfrac{m}{s^{2}} }$$
    Question: People often get velocity and acceleration confused. Explain the difference between them without math.
    answer

    Velocity is how fast you are going. It's how quick you change your position. Velocity doesn't feel like anything, but it looks like stuff going past you.

    Acceleration is how fast your speed changes. It's your change in velocity. You can feel acceleration in your body. It feels like being pushed. If you are falling it feels like butterflies.

    Example: I start a velocity of 1 m/s. I speed up to 3 m/s over 10 seconds. What is my acceleration?
    solution
    $$\Delta v = v_{f}-v_{i}$$ $$\Delta v = 3 \,\mathrm{ \tfrac{m}{s}} -1\, \mathrm{ \tfrac{m}{s} }$$ $$\Delta v = 2\, \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$
    $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a = \frac{2\, \mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}} }{10\, \mathrm{s} }$$ $$a = 0.2\,\mathrm{ \tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    Example: A small camera drone starts 2 m above the ground while filming a school field. It rises to 14 m above the ground, and the camera records for 9 s. What is its vertical displacement?
    solution $$\Delta x=x_f-x_i$$ $$\Delta x=14-2$$ $$\Delta x=12\,\mathrm{m}$$

    The drone ends 12 m higher than it started. How long it filmed doesn't change its displacement.

    Example: A student starts at a locker marked -12 m and walks to a classroom marked 35 m. What is the student's displacement?
    solution $$\Delta x=x_f-x_i$$ $$\Delta x=35-(-12)$$ $$\Delta x=47\,\mathrm{m}$$

    Subtracting a negative position adds. The student walks 12 m to reach the 0 m mark, then 35 m more.

    Example: A runner sprints 0.10 km east, turns around, and jogs 35 m west. What are the runner's distance traveled and displacement?
    solution $$0.10\,\mathrm{km} \left(\frac{1000\,\mathrm{m}}{1\,\mathrm{km}}\right) = 100\,\mathrm{m}$$

    Distance traveled counts the whole path.

    $$d=100+35$$ $$d=135\,\mathrm{m}$$

    Displacement only compares the final position to the initial position. Let east be positive.

    $$\Delta x=100-35$$ $$\Delta x=65\,\mathrm{m}$$

    The runner covered 135 m, but only ended up 65 m east of the start.

    Example: A city bus leaves the science museum stop at 7:48 AM and follows a 6.2 km route to school, arriving at 8:13 AM. How long was the ride in minutes?
    solution

    The hour changes during the ride, so count the minutes up to 8:00, then the minutes after 8:00.

    $$\Delta t=12\,\mathrm{min}+13\,\mathrm{min}$$ $$\Delta t=25\,\mathrm{min}$$

    The route length doesn't matter here. The clock times alone give the time period.

    Example: A runner jogs for 12 s and covers 80 m on the school's running track. What is the runner's displacement?
    solution

    This cannot be solved from the information given. 80 m is the distance traveled, the length of the path. Displacement needs the starting and ending positions. A track curves, so unless the runner stayed on a straight section, the displacement is less than 80 m. We also don't know its direction.

    Example: A skateboarder goes from the 0.008 km mark to the 68 m mark in 15 s. What is the skateboarder's average velocity?
    solution $$0.008\,\mathrm{km} \left(\frac{1000\,\mathrm{m}}{1\,\mathrm{km}}\right) = 8\,\mathrm{m}$$ $$\Delta x=x_f-x_i$$ $$\Delta x=68-8$$ $$\Delta x=60\,\mathrm{m}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{60}{15}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=4\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$

    On average, the skateboarder moves 4 m farther along the path every second.

    Example: A dog runs 18 m east, then 6 m west, in 8 s. What are the dog's distance traveled and average velocity?
    solution $$d=18+6$$ $$d=24\,\mathrm{m}$$

    Let east be positive.

    $$\Delta x=18-6$$ $$\Delta x=12\,\mathrm{m}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{12}{8}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=1.5\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$

    Average velocity uses the 12 m displacement, not the 24 m the dog ran. The answer is 1.5 m/s east.

    Example: An elevator rises with an average velocity of 1.5 m/s. How long does it take to rise 36 m?
    solution $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$1.5=\frac{36}{\Delta t}$$ $$1.5\Delta t=36$$ $$\Delta t=\frac{36}{1.5}$$ $$\Delta t=24\,\mathrm{s}$$

    36 m is about 10 floors, so a 24 s ride seems reasonable.

    Example: A train backs up slowly at 5.4 km/h for 20 s while coupling to another car. Let forward be positive. What is the train's displacement?
    solution $$5.4\left(\frac{\mathrm{\color{#f05}{km}}}{\mathrm{\color{#07b}{h}}}\right)\left(\frac{1000\,\mathrm{m}}{1\,\mathrm{\color{#f05}{km}}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{\color{#07b}{h}}}{3600\,\mathrm{s}}\right) = 1.5\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$

    The train moves backward, so its velocity is negative.

    $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=-1.5\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$-1.5=\frac{\Delta x}{20}$$ $$(-1.5)(20)=\Delta x$$ $$\Delta x=-30\,\mathrm{m}$$

    The negative sign means the train ends 30 m behind where it started.

    Example: A position vs. time graph goes through the points (2 s, 5 m) and (8 s, 29 m). What is the slope of the graph?
    solution

    The slope of a position vs. time graph is velocity.

    $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{29-5}{8-2}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{24}{6}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=4\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$

    The position increases by 4 m every second, so the object moves in the positive direction.

    Example: A position vs. time graph falls from 40 m at 0 s to -20 m at 10 s. What average velocity does the graph show?
    solution $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{-20-40}{10-0}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{-60}{10}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=-6\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$

    A downward slope means a negative velocity. The object moves in the negative direction and passes right through the 0 m mark.

    Example: A skateboarder speeds up from 1.5 m/s to 7.5 m/s in 4 s. What is the skateboarder's average acceleration?
    solution $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{7.5-1.5}{4}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=1.5\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    The skateboarder's velocity increases by 1.5 m/s every second.

    Example: A bike is moving forward at 9 m/s and comes to rest in 3 s. What is the bike's average acceleration?
    solution $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{0-9}{3}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=-3\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    The acceleration points backward, opposite the bike's motion, so the bike slows down. It loses 3 m/s every second.

    Example: A cart is moving left at 4 m/s, then 5 s later it is moving right at 6 m/s. Let right be positive. What is the cart's average acceleration?
    solution $$v_i=-4\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$ $$v_f=6\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{6-(-4)}{5}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=2\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    The velocity changed by 10 m/s, not 2 m/s. The acceleration points right the whole time, even while the cart is still moving left and slowing down.

    Example: A velocity vs. time graph goes through the points (3 s, 2 m/s) and (11 s, -6 m/s). What is the slope of the graph?
    solution

    The slope of a velocity vs. time graph is acceleration.

    $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{-6-2}{11-3}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{-8}{8}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=-1\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    The velocity drops 1 m/s every second. It reaches zero at 5 s, so the object stops and turns around before speeding up in the negative direction.

    Example: A cyclist on a straight bike path starts a timer at the 0.020 km mark and is at the 170 m mark after 0.50 min. During that same time, the bike computer reports that the cyclist's velocity changes from 2 m/s to 8 m/s. Find the displacement, average velocity, and average acceleration.
    solution $$0.020\,\mathrm{km} \left(\frac{1000\,\mathrm{m}}{1\,\mathrm{km}}\right) = 20\,\mathrm{m}$$ $$0.50\,\mathrm{min} \left(\frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{1\,\mathrm{min}}\right) = 30\,\mathrm{s}$$
    $$\Delta x=x_f-x_i$$ $$\Delta x=170-20$$ $$\Delta x=150\,\mathrm{m}$$
    $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{150}{30}$$ $$v_{\mathrm{avg}}=5\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s}}$$
    $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{8-2}{30}$$ $$a_{\mathrm{avg}}=0.2\,\mathrm{\tfrac{m}{s^{2}}}$$

    The numbers agree with each other. If the cyclist speeds up steadily from 2 m/s to 8 m/s, the average velocity should be halfway between them, 5 m/s.

    Example: Two cars drive toward each other on a straight east-west road. Let east be positive. At the same instant, one car is at the 0 m marker and travels east at a constant 20 m/s. The other car is at the 600 m marker and travels west at a constant 10 m/s. How long until the cars meet?
    solution

    Rearrange the velocity equation to find each car's final position.

    $$v=\frac{x_f-x_i}{\Delta t}$$ $$x_f=x_i+v\Delta t$$

    Write one position equation for each car. The westbound car has a negative velocity.

    $$x_1=0+20\Delta t$$ $$x_2=600-10\Delta t$$

    At the meeting time, both cars have the same position.

    $$20\Delta t=600-10\Delta t$$ $$30\Delta t=600$$ $$\Delta t=20\,\mathrm{s}$$

    The cars meet after 20 s, at the 400 m marker. The eastbound car is faster, so it covers more of the gap.

    Reading (5 minutes): Read The Burden of Skepticism by Carl Sagan from Skeptical Inquirer. Then answer these questions.

    What does Sagan's used-car example show about skepticism in everyday life?
    answer

    Skepticism means checking a claim before accepting it when there is a cost to being wrong. It can include asking questions, looking for evidence, or consulting someone who understands the evidence better.


    Why does Sagan argue that a claim should receive extra scrutiny when it strongly appeals to hope, fear, or grief?
    answer

    Strong emotions can make people easier to mislead or more willing to accept a comforting claim without evidence. He argues that emotional importance is a reason to ask more careful questions, not fewer.
